# Chapter 19: Deals with Harbin, and Sister Kaella, and no deal with Jenna

Date: 07-03 of Tarsakh 1495

## Summary
Vukradin was still fuming over Harbin’s accusations when Ser Kaelen arrived to congratulate him on being cleared, in terms that made everything worse. He was delighted the name was cleared, had told Harbin that a member of the Order of the Gauntlet could not commit such a crime, and considered the accusation scandalous. Vukradin rolled his eyes and echoed the word back at him; Ser Kaelen either did not hear or chose not to, and continued about having insisted on an immediate trial to clear the name, because the integrity of the Order had been questioned and that was unacceptable. “Very,” Vukradin sneered. The knight then turned to the real target: he was very frustrated with the leadership of this town, Harbin had been a terrible representative of Lord Neverember, he had made scandalous accusations against members of the order, and a town meeting was essential.

Harbin, standing nearby, tried to slow it down with a tremor in his voice — let’s not be too hasty, perhaps a polite and civil discussion, perhaps a town meeting, a gathering to discuss some of the events. Then he turned to Ser Kaelen with a hint of steel and named the actual constraint: “His Lordship appoints the position of the Town Master, and his Lordship has the privilege and only he has the privilege of replacing me.” Vukradin could not believe what he was hearing. “No democracy in Phandalin, that’s an outrage! Absurd!” He felt the power of the people rising in him and reached the obvious conclusion — “We need a revolution!” — at which Ser Kaelen went pale and whispered a request that they perhaps avoid the R word. Vukradin pressed his grievance instead: all he heard were rumours and innuendo trashing his good name, and he pointed at Harbin with the accusation that mattered most to him. “He is using blood money!” The Townmaster turned pale and stuttered about a tiny little misunderstanding, and Brewbarry stepped close to him to make the discomfort complete.

At which point Harbin conceded only that a town gathering could be discussed. But when Vukradin asked for it to be convened, Harbin discovered reasons it could not happen now — such meetings cannot be held from one minute to the next, they need preparations, an agenda, discussion of the format, pre-work. He reminded Vukradin of every agent who had failed to find him a gig, and this time Vukradin would not tolerate it: “Now or pitchforks. Do you want pitchforks?” Then he realised he did not need Harbin at all. He could convene his own meeting.

Ser Kaelen pulled him aside as he prepared to call one and end the Townmaster. His dedication to participatory democracy, power to the people, and opposition to authoritarianism was commendable — but they live in the real world, and in the real world this territory belongs to Lord Neverember. Kaelen warned against disrupting Neverember’s authority and proposed participatory democracy with Harbin left as the figurehead Townmaster. Vukradin, who wanted it so badly, could manage only “But he’s corrupt!” Ser Kaelen’s counter-offer was a privy council that made the decisions for Harbin. Vukradin refused to be shut down and declared the town meeting anyway.

He marched onto the town green and started playing, and the notables assembled: Elmar, the owner of Barthen’s provisions, with his two clerks Ander and Thistle; Halia Thornton of the Miner’s Exchange; Linene of the Lionshield Coster; Spiderman; and Adabra. Farmers and miners he had never met came too, though the town is small enough that past twenty or so there are not many more. So he started a concert, and of course the crowd began chanting for Valphine.

Scanning them, he noticed a woman he had never seen, staring at him: late thirties, with an air of refined, unassuming elegance, auburn hair tied back in a neat intricate knot, green eyes, and pleasant, easily forgettable features. She wore practical, high-quality attire and carried a fine leather satchel. He smiled at her, because she is a voter. She smirked.

He was about to give his speech about the people and freedom when someone screamed “DRAGON!” Vukradin’s response was to denounce the rumour as a form of societal control, on the grounds that there is no dragon — and then to turn around and discover that what everyone was pointing at was, in fact, a dragon. All he could think was that he had thought it was a rumour. The white dragon was diving toward them with, he assumed, food in mind. Harbin started screaming that this is what happens when adventurers talk instead of doing their job, and Vukradin turned it around: when they kill the dragon, it will be even more proof of how incapable Harbin is at doing his.

He then did what he always does, which is to compose. Every revolution begins with a heroic and impossible last stand; he thought about the ballads that would be written about his heroism in staring down and killing this dragon, considered a few couplets and musical elements, and concluded that only an opera could convey the religious and political energy of the moment. One thought remained: if he killed the dragon, the fates would prove he was destined for this change. Brewbarry planted himself in front of him — “I’ll protect you” — and then, watching it come, asked the question nobody else had: should we not ask what he wants? Vukradin told him he was welcome to ask. He had a different plan, and as the dragon approached he cast phantasmal force, making the dragon believe it had secured prey. He held his breath, and the dragon flew away, convinced it had its prey and feeling the spell’s pain, to go and eat its phantasmal dinner.

Then a voice called for an announcement, and Harbin surprised him. The recent actions of Vukradin, the Townmaster said, demonstrate the need for a more active and engaged community in decision-making — and while it was not in his power to make Vukradin Townmaster, a review of the charter allowed a new privy council of himself, Toblen, Elmar, Halia, Linene and Adabra, together with “our new hero Vukradin.” Vukradin was tempted to simply seize the Townmaster’s job, and Ser Kaelen could see it, and explained again that Lord Neverember would read it as an attack on his authority and send troops, and that even if the troops were stopped the harm to the town would be considerable. For a moment the nobility of revolution called. Then Vukradin decided that compromise is also noble.

Valphine had spent the day elsewhere. While the others went into town she stayed outside it with Corbin and Locutus, planning to slip in after dark to meet Sister Kaella and learn what she could. The white dragon’s roar nearly changed that — she told Corbin to watch Locutus and ran for Phandalin — but the dragon flew off before she got far. So she waited for night, and before going she stripped away every sign of Lathander and made herself look as much as possible like a drow of the Underdark. Locutus looked terrified, which she thought was good.

He led her to an abandoned, decrepit building at the far edge of town. Inside, a woman in a dark robe and hood stepped out of the shadows and whispered a question, pointing at Valphine: who is that? “I am his assistant.” The woman turned to Locutus and asked whether he had killed Borg. Locutus nodded, and Valphine produced the head. And then, before she could react, Sister Kaella struck Locutus down — her first two rapier strikes missed, and her offhand dagger cut the orc down where he stood. Valphine was ecstatic; it reminded her of the games she used to play with her sisters in Menzoberranzan. What shocked her was what came next: “Now, Valphine, we can have a private conversation.” She managed only “Sure?”

Sister Kaella removed her hood — cold grey eyes, hair cut short, a scar near her neck, armour under the cloak, and the rapier and bloodied dagger held pointed at Valphine as a reminder of the consequences of disagreement. She opened with a confession of priorities: she has more important business than working for the Carver. Then the failure: “The Borg had one job: kill Big Al. Leave no witnesses. If he had done that, my carefully laid plan would have worked. But he didn’t. And so the plot to blame you for Big Al’s death has failed.” Valphine suggested she might work for someone else, and Kaella smiled: “The Carver’s gang needs new management.”

Then came the misreading that shaped the whole conversation. “I can see that you are exactly the kind of person I can work with,” Kaella said — and Valphine realised she was being read as a racial stereotype, the duplicitous, backstabbing, evil drow, exactly as Locutus had assumed she would sell him into the Underdark. How droll. Oblivious, Kaella laid out the plan: in a few days she would supply information to kill the Carver’s next lieutenant. She anticipated the obvious question about why they don’t simply kill the Carver, and answered it: “Because he has a very unique ability to communicate with his people, he can communicate with us telepathically. I don’t know how.” Kaella said the Carver normally avoided appearing in person; she hoped killing a lieutenant would force him into the open. Valphine, recalling the stories of mind flayers, aboleths and eldritch monsters that can do this, decided privately that this was a bridge too far and was on the point of withdrawing.

Kaella, still not sensing it, asked whether they had an arrangement, and explained her own motive when Valphine wondered why she was making the offer at all: the Carver does not like failure, she made a promise and failed to deliver, and she fears retaliation from the Carver’s remaining lieutenants. She needs to disappear. “If you kill his lieutenants, then the Carver will come out of hiding, and then I can kill them.” A pause. “And by I, I mean you.” Then the probe: “Those people you are with are they your allies, your dupes? What exactly is your role?” Valphine called them her musical troupe, then her assistance, and Kaella believed her — and went further: “Interesting. This whole Lathander thing is a front, right? You are clearly a cleric of Lolth.” Valphine glared. Kaella smiled as though sharing a secret, told her there was no need to say anything, and then hardened: Kaella dismissed Vukradin as naive and simple-minded, Brewbarry for his “goody-goody two-shoes nonsense,” and began an unclear thought about Soma being “obsessed.” She remained convinced she could make a deal with Valphine. She tossed a bag at Valphine’s feet — the money Locutus was to have received for killing Borg, “payment for working together” — and disappeared before Valphine could react.

Back at the tavern, Brewbarry watched the woman who had smirked on the green during the dragon attack and was still staring at Vukradin. He asked Spiderman where she was from and learned she was from Neverwinter and worked for the Lord’s Alliance. Asked how he knew, Spiderman whispered that they have a stench and she carries their insignia. Brewbarry approached her suspiciously and questioned her purpose.

She saw him coming, smiled, and asked who he was. “Brewbarry,” he growled. She softened her voice to settle him and asked whether he had been a big fan of Vukradin’s in Neverwinter, attending all his performances; he nodded. Then she said something he did not expect — that she thinks the music is fantastic, and that it is a terrible shame so few people know about it. Confused, he asked what she meant and what she suggested doing about it, and got the ask: “I would love an introduction. And I’ve noticed how Vukradin has made new friends.” His voice turned threatening. She reassured him again, mentioning rumours that “she’s a friend of the Order of the Gauntlet.” Asked why she cared, she looked startled and produced the flattering answer: she hears Vukradin is very committed to conflict-free treasure and only wants to work with high-integrity individuals. Brewbarry agreed that should be the way for all of us. She smirked and described her own role — not one to judge, but concerned with law and order and “the preservation of society... in the face of chaos and the mayhem that surrounds us all.” Brewbarry knew the kind, and asked whether that meant preserving her own interests.

Taken aback, she said she should not be surprised to hear that from — a pause — “Uthgardt,” her tone dripping contempt. She then steered the conversation back to Vukradin and his choices, particularly his choice to work for the Order of the Gauntlet — and asking whether he had not received a letter recently. Brewbarry asked how that was any of her business, and she laughed: “Because I sent it.” Confused as to why she would ask about a letter she sent, he pressed, and she explained: she still sees him working with Ser Kaelen, it is her understanding that Vukradin opposes blood money, and “I’m sure that even Vukradin, in his infinite musical wisdom, would know that House Margaster is only blood money.” She had meant to approach him herself, but since his biggest fan had come to her, an introduction would do. Brewbarry’s terms were simple: “Hand over your weapons.” She smiled and dropped a dagger, a rapier, and a second dagger on the table, remarking softly that she was not there to commit acts of violence but to have a pleasant conversation. When he pressed the point about the weapons she pointed at his halberd, and he nodded and went to fetch the bard.

Vukradin was doubtful until he heard the woman had written the mysterious letter, at which point he insisted on the meeting; Brewbarry told him she had been disarmed before he reached the table. Vukradin thanked him and reminded him that a tavern is an unlikely place for violence, which Brewbarry received without conviction — “I like your confidence.” What actually enthused Vukradin was hearing that she loved his music and thought he should be more famous.

Seated with a beer from Spiderman, he heard her opening — she had been looking all over for him — and told her he had received her letter. She was confused, and moved to the point: given his well-known position on immoral money, she was shocked to find he continues to work for the Order of the Gauntlet. Vukradin’s reply reframed everything: “Define work for?” She cited Ser Kaelen, who says Vukradin is a full-fledged member. He sipped his beer: “I am friends with many people. I have many fans. I spread my music to all types.” Then he turned it into a sales opportunity — was she interested in his music? Surely her organization would love to hear it. “I want to be friends with everyone.” She spluttered that Ser Kaelen insists he is a member, and asked whether she was to understand this was not an exclusive arrangement. Vukradin asked whether this was a trial. She looked perplexed, as though the idea that he might not commit to one group were the strangest thing in the world, and he explained himself plainly: he is just trying to make organic fair trade gold so he can start his music studio and spread his music to all of Faerun, and he is happy to be friends with lots of people, “as long as they’re not evil, and as long as we earn our money fairly and justly.”

That finally made her smile. She was very relieved to find he had not embraced Ser Kaelen’s philosophy wholeheartedly and instead took the appropriate musical view of wanting to work with everyone — and she now understood why, after receiving that missive about House Margaster and their funding of the Order of the Gauntlet, he had not abandoned them: he treated his full membership as friendship rather than an exclusive commitment. Her tone worried him and he asked again whether he was on trial. She laughed and reassured him: she was asking because she had come to make him an offer, a suggestion, a potential way for him to become — what was the word — more famous. He smiled. She began to introduce herself as a member of the Lord’s Alliance, and he interrupted to ask what the Lord’s Alliance is. Her jaw dropped at the honest question. He followed it with “Is it like the Zhentarim?”, which shocked her further, and explained that he had heard of the Zhentarim but not met them either, and asked whether she was with them. She stared, then smiled: “I had heard so much about you that your passion for music had made you oblivious to the outside world, and I see now it is true.”

Brewbarry grunted and asked about her weapons, and she answered him with contempt, mangling his name — “Blue beerrree? Is that your name? Did I mispronounce it?” Vukradin did not know why she mispronounced his friend’s name. Brewbarry returned it in kind, asking whether “GEEENA” was the correct pronunciation of hers, and Vukradin interjected with “JINA!” She laughed: “It is Jenna, but for you, Vukradin, it can be anything.” Asked why she would want her name confused, she said it was because she only cares about his success — and it hit him. She wanted to be his agent. And he does need an agent. She smiled with her whole face and made the pitch: she could get him plugged in to all of the top places in Waterdeep, have him performing in front of the nobility, and was sure she could even get him an audience to play a set for Lord Neverember himself. He nodded with a hint of excitement, but not too much.

At that point he heard Valphine’s Lathanderian Choral Death Chant mix start up, and tried hard not to focus on it — the crowd that had demanded her a few minutes earlier was now chanting his name. He told Jenna he needed to get back on stage, because his friend was bombing. She stopped him for one small detail about his fair-trade gold. He said he was a real believer in it. She looked hurt and clarified that she did not want him to abandon the philosophy, only to refine it: the treasure is stolen, but stolen from the people rather than from a person, and let the adventurers be the first to reclaim it. He might think this another theft, and it would be — except that by taking the treasure and redistributing it as purchases, the adventurer enables the community to grow through the stimulus of the new gold. “In short, by purchasing goods, you are returning the gold to the **people** from whom it was stolen.”

Vukradin shook his head. “Yeah, no. This is not a line I’m gonna cross. I gotta go perform.” And then, plainly: “Sorry, not interested in compromising my morals, I’m strong on this one. We need to return these items to their rightful owners.” She protested that she could have done so much for him. He turned on the charm instead of closing the door, suggesting she reconsider, because it would be a good arrangement for her — he could really entertain her nobles, and they would think highly of her as his agent. He charmed her without changing her position; she was no longer upset and might even have admired his dedication to principle, but she remained committed to her mission.

As the evening wound down, two local farmers approached Valphine and asked whether she was truly Valphine of the painful light. They had heard that she taught that light creates pain, and through pain come salvation and glory. Delighted that they understood the doctrine, Valphine asked whether they were true believers and declared that she was ready to start her order.
