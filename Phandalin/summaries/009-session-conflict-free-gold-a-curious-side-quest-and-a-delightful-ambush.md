# Chapter 9: Conflict-free gold, a curious side quest, and a delightful ambush

Date: 03-02 to 07-02 of Taraskh 1495

## Summary
The party’s departure for the Tower of Storms was interrupted almost immediately. Rounding a bend, they found a small trade cart overturned and half-swallowed by undergrowth, and huddled behind it two badly shaken travellers — Ellarrah, a middle-aged human merchant, and Jarek, her groom — with their pack horses nowhere in sight. Vukradin, taking stock of the rescue party bearing down on these terrified strangers (an aasimar, an Uthgardt barbarian, a drow, a large tortle, and one human who looked like a bandit in armour stripped off a corpse), stepped forward to be reassuring and to see whether a coin could be earned here without anyone having to die for it. Ellarrah’s opening line — “Orcs attacked us” — was answered by Soma in her guttural, patronizing, motherly register: “We aren’t orcs.” Vukradin wanted to scream that this was obvious. Asked who they actually were, Valphine got there first with a single shouted word, “Lathander!”, which Ellarrah received as devotion — “Oh, Priestess! The faith must be strong in you!” — and Valphine confirmed with the beatific look of the widely misunderstood: “He’s painful!” Ellarrah admitted she had never heard that teaching.

Calmed, the merchants explained they had been bound for Phandalin. Soma offered to mend the broken cart and Ellarrah accepted; Ellarrah then asked whether anyone could recover a horse that had bolted down into a field, and Brewbarry — a brute, but a loyal one, and never happier than with a task involving strength or livestock — went after it. With him gone, Jarek finally spoke his piece: “I told you we should travel with some protection.”

Pressed for the details of the attack, Ellarrah’s account turned strange in the telling. The orcs had moved “with unnerving discipline not like the usual pack I have seen,” and had carried odd markings — “like a swirl, or an eye?” Her eyes darted as she reached for the memory, then she shook her head: “No, it’s gone now.” Jarek described the same eerie coordination and added his own detail, offered almost apologetically: “I think... I heard something... a sort of hum, not really a sound, just... in my head, before they struck. Then it was gone.” Valphine said mind flayers. Vukradin dismissed her, on the grounds that she always says mind flayers.

Brewbarry returned with the horse and Ellarrah, relieved, offered a reward. Vukradin held out his hand. Ellarrah reached past it to Valphine: “For the Morninglord and whomever you feel is worthy.” Vukradin took the lesson rather than the slight — “This! This is Fair Trade and Conflict Free gold” — and, over the grumbling of a party that wanted to press on, insisted they escort the merchants all the way back to Phandalin. They did.

In town they found Ser Kaelen Thorn still in residence, and Vukradin reported the whole affair to him. Ser Kaelen was pleased that the bard was not only chasing the big picture but doing small good along the way, and said it aligned with the broader values of the Order of the Gauntlet, finishing with the line Vukradin most wanted to hear: “Bringing you into the fold was the right thing to do.” Compliments, Vukradin noted, are always good. Since he had been asked to commission music for the Order, he needed to know its style, and asked to hear a cheer of the Gauntlet. Ser Kaelen stammered, apologized, and insisted Vukradin’s talents would improve anything the Order had ever done — but Vukradin pressed, and the knight shouted it out, mortified: “Steel and shield, Heart and band. We stand as one. A steadfast band.” Vukradin, probing for range, asked whether the Order had magic users. It did. “This chant is rather embarrassing, that’s why we need your genius!” Someone, at last, who appreciated his vision.

Walking out of Phandalin, Vukradin took stock. Getting Ellarrah and Jarek home safely and being paid for it was exactly the kind of coin he had been looking for — earned through genuine assistance rather than “barbaric slaughtering” — proof that he could fund his studio without compromising a single principle. Like a well-structured song, the rescue had resolved a discordant world into harmony, and he said to no one in particular that “even in a world of monsters and mayhem, opportunities exist for a principled artist to earn a livelihood without compromising their ideals.”

Passing Barthen’s Provisions on the road toward the Triboar Trail, they were intercepted by Adabra, who smiled at Vukradin and then pointedly drew Soma aside. Vukradin approached anyway, was rebuffed, and eavesdropped instead. The old grove past Whispering Grove — once vibrant, a true blessing of Chauntea — was withering unnaturally: “Trees twist in impossible shapes, colors bleeding wrong, and the creatures... they’re not right. Too many eyes, jerky movements, lost and broken.” Adabra muttered about interventionists and the Emerald Enclave. Soma listened carefully and agreed to find out what was happening. Vukradin’s only comment was a hope that she had asked for money.

The Triboar Trail took several days and was curiously bereft of both travellers and corpses. At the High Road they stopped for the night at the Wayside Inn, now run by Martisha Vinetalker, a striking half-elf whose pleasing features sit oddly against a cold, calculating, and charismatic manner. The bartender was Backes Dunfield, a scarred human with a crooked smile; the cook, Cooragh Struckt, was famous for stews and meat pies that were both incredibly tasty and alarmingly well-spiced. They did not disappoint. In the morning Martisha pointed the party toward a trail to the coast.

The trail snaked through the broken, hilly ground of the Sword Coast and at intervals dropped them into unnatural gullies that split the earth in two. Vukradin, ever the pedant, observed that this would only be possible if a river ran through here, and that the country was far too dry for a river. Valphine drew a different conclusion, and a theological one: she wondered how these overbrighters survive at all without “the Faerzress” to shape the land for attackers and defenders alike, decided that whatever had cracked the ground was magic rather than any natural phenomenon, and saw in Lathander’s extravagance of light and land the sloppiness that breeds war and suffering. She felt a pleasant shiver, and thanked Vukradin for having shown her more of the fiery god’s plan.

One of these paths narrowed to a tight juncture, and Valphine’s drow-bred instinct for impending ill prickled. Brewbarry confirmed it: subtle tracks, and the tell-tale glint of concealed armour. Rather than walk into it, the party climbed out of the gully, took the high ground, and came at their would-be ambushers from behind. Valphine’s first assessment — “Bah, orcs, barely worthy of slaughter” — did not survive a closer look. These were far more organized than the chaotic brutes of the surface world: they had planned to drop a rock to seal the party’s exit and then pour fire down into a tight valley with no easy escape, and they had a shaman or cleric among them. Straining to follow their guttural chatter, Valphine’s knowledge of the Orc tongue gave her the plan’s premise — the party were to be an “easy kill” — and with it the question of who had told them so.

The fight opened with Vukradin’s Sleep, a peculiarly gentle form of aggression that dropped one orc and the shaman together. Soma transformed into a brown bear and tore into two more with brutal elegance. Brewbarry embraced his rage, went in head-on, and shrugged off what came back at him. Valphine’s hand crossbow snapped, and then she called down Faerie Fire — the gloriously painful light of Lathander outlining their foes and granting the party advantage, burning away not only their shapes but their illusion of safety. She found it deeply satisfying, and arrived mid-battle at the doctrine she had been circling all chapter: “Being good is a form of pain against my very nature. How delightful, that to be good is to cause pain, which is to cause pleasure.” In the middle of the chaos the orc shaman revealed a trick of his own and shifted into a boar — a familiar move to anyone raised near the Underdark’s lesser shapeshifters, but a delightful surprise all the same. The chapter closes with the battle still running, and with Valphine thoroughly enthralled by it: an ambush with unexpected tactical depth, a rare and exquisitely bitter spice in an otherwise bland dish.
