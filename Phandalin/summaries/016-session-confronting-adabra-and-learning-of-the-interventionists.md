# Chapter 16: Confronting Adabra and learning of the Interventionists

Date: Tarsakh 1495 (exact day not stated on the recording)

## Summary
Soma came into town feeling like “a shell sprout, wet from the nest, a child that has been taken advantage of.” She had trusted Adabra, and that trust had been used to make her stop the people who were trying to save her plane. Vukradin tried to calm her and got a growl for it — “I want to chew her out!” — and a shrug was all he had to offer in return. They went to the house Adabra was borrowing from Sister Garaele, with Lyra and her three surviving followers accompanying them.

Adabra opened her door smiling. Soma, furious, opened with nothing more precise than “What the hell! What was that!” — and the kind woman went cold. What came back was slow, patronizing, and dripping with contempt: she had held such hope for Soma, had thought she heard the true whisper of the world, and instead Soma had let the interventionists fill her head with noise. She waved a hand at Lyra as she said it. “They speak of cleansing and planar infections because they fear what they cannot control. You help them wound the world to make themselves feel righteous.” Soma, chastened, reached for the technicality that was also true: they hadn’t helped, the ritual had not been completed, and in some sense it had failed because of her. “You could say we interrupted the interventionists.” Adabra, confused, pointed at Lyra and asked the obvious question — then why are they here? Soma explained that the party had saved them, because “they didn’t deserve to die.” Adabra grew suspicious and asked directly whether they had tried to stop the ritual. Soma tried to lie, tried to deceive her, and found herself too old for the games hatchlings play.

So she looked at Vukradin, on the theory that the silver-tongued bard can convince anyone of anything, and he confused Adabra just enough that the betrayal slipped out of view. Asked about the treant, Soma answered, “In stable condition. Holding steady,” and Vukradin backed her deception. Adabra took that to mean alive. The party had felled it but remained unsure whether it was dead or what the planar tear had done to it. Lyra could not stand it and screamed, “You are insane!”, which Vukradin met with an offer of a beer. Soma then asked him to sell the upside: not all was lost, everyone got something, the planar rift was not sealed and the interventionists were alive. He went to work, and soon Adabra and Lyra were no longer on the edge of blows. When Lyra wound up for a rant, he deflected again — “Let’s play music” — but this time she had had enough: “If you cannot see the evil this woman represents and her fanaticism and its danger, then you’re a fool.”

Vukradin’s own account of the scene is simpler. He wants to be friends with everyone, and does not understand why people are so full of hate. He had once believed the key was to play music better than his audience; he now believes it is to play popular music, because he just wants them to love him. The Lyra–Adabra feud is inconvenient precisely because he wants to work with everyone — except the Zhentarim, who do not fit his policy of taking money only from conflict-free treasure. As Lyra stormed off, her three followers reluctantly followed; Vukradin invited them for a drink, and they promised to return later. “I can’t stay now, but we’ll be back later if you are buying.” He accepted that casually, and the group later presumed Toblen would cover the drinks.

Adabra stayed and kept going: “The interventionists started this war on nature when they chose to reject the multiverse’s diversity. Their rituals and cleanses are acts of violence.” Vukradin’s response was to shout “Boooring,” then “Boring,” then “Everyone is so serious!” Taken aback, she tried an angle she thought he could not refuse — he likes music, and isn’t music an explosion of insight and beauty reaching from any point in the universe? Is there bad music, or is there only music? What the interventionists believe, she said, is that some music is bad just because they don’t like it. Valphine smiled her peculiar smile and supplied the counter-example: “It’s bad because it crosses planar boundaries!” And the argument, which might have worked on Vukradin a few days earlier, failed on the one he had since learned. “There is music that is bad,” he explained, having concluded that playing music people love is the essential art. Soma nodded and extended it: “Like, there are some planes that are bad for this one.” Adabra, charmed anyway, allowed that “There is hope for you, Vukradin, and perhaps even Soma?” Soma shook her head.

Seeing the opening, Vukradin asked why he and his fellow adventurers had been dragged into an internal feud of the Emerald Enclave. What he gathered was the shape of the whole conflict: within the Enclave, *interventionist* is the worst thing you can call anyone, because every member believes they are preserving nature rather than intervening in it. His own conclusion was characteristically personal — he does not want to be an interventionist, because being an interventionist means fewer fans. Adabra talked on, and bored, he heard her describe her side’s position: those who see the true harm of the interventionists “have been reduced to begging for crumbs of resources, for being cast out and treated like we are mad and do not understand what is going on.” She had been forced — forced! — to reach out to any ally she could find, believing that he and Soma understood the universal beauty of the multiverse. Then the revision: “I was wrong about Soma, but perhaps I was right about you.” Vukradin invited her to come hear him play.

Before the party left, Adabra asked whether the treant was still there. Soma was visibly working to say nothing, waiting for Vukradin to answer. He weighed a half-truth, decided “It’s still there?” would only provoke follow-ups, and told the truth instead: he wasn’t really sure what happened, there had been a massive planar tear, and the treant had been right in the middle of it. Valphine then mentioned the creature, which Vukradin recognised as a gift — it pulled Adabra clean off her line of inquiry. Once Valphine had described its strange nature and behaviour, Adabra went into something like ecstasy: “A symptom of the process, not the cause. It was likely drawn to the energy, a planar magpie. Did it… did you kill it too? Or did you stop to wonder what its purpose was in the grand design?” Vukradin answered, “I didn’t intervene,” and Valphine echoed, “We didn’t intervene,” Adabra then handed Soma five gold pieces for the party. She carried on for some time in that ecstatic register about planes and designs and universalism, which Vukradin found boring; Soma told him afterwards that it had been meaningless nonsense, and he worked hard not to say that he already knew.

Valphine’s read on Adabra was colder and more familiar: a zealot, of a kind she has seen worshipping Lolth — convinced there is some greater purpose to the universe, that the demonic powers they serve will grant them power, or that in serving them they serve something enduring. Fools. The only such thing, in her view, is Lathander: a god who every day shows his power by pushing the darkness away, who is strong enough to keep the drow underground, and who causes so much exquisite pain.

The party’s other problem was liquidity. Vukradin muttered to no one that if they sold the pelts, Adabra would know what they had done — but they needed the money. Corbin offered to find a fence, and swore it would not be blood money; Vukradin was not convinced. They tried Barthen’s Provisions first, where Barthen looked the goods over and offered a paltry sum, not seeing what Vukradin saw in them. The Lionshield Coster went very differently. Linene Graywind, the thirty-five-year-old woman managing it, was visibly excited and pressed question after question about where and how the pelts had been found — and Vukradin, whom Valphine considered a fool for it, answered truthfully, giving away information that could have been ransomed for gold. Linene then offered more than twice Barthen’s price. The table connected that interest to background about the Lionshield Coster: the Lionshield Coster relied on a Commission-operated interplanar shipping device, and it had somehow stopped working. She was tempted to push for more, but Vukradin accepted the price. Linene took the pelts and remarked that her bosses would be very interested in this particular planar effect. On the way out she asked whether they would perform that night, and — to Valphine’s gratification — asked specifically whether Valphine would play. Vukradin was annoyed, then remembered his goal of being beloved by all, and reasoned that there is no point arguing when you can say yes and profit.

The set at the Stonehill Tavern was off. Vukradin performed strongly, but Valphine’s weaker contribution and the mismatch between his pop direction and her exotic style confused the audience. The songs were received less well than last time, and partway through the Emerald Enclave’s agents arrived and — at Vukradin’s invitation — sat at the party’s table. He opened with “So are you sticking with Lyra?” One of the three drank the beer Toblen had brought and answered that they had sworn their lives and honour to the Emerald Enclave, that Lyra had merely been upset, and that her goal had been to save the tree and the forest, and all of it had failed.

Soma, sitting beside the paragons Meril had told her protected the forests and the swamps, wanted to understand. She asked what had been riding on the ritual. Vukradin, mid-gulp, put it more plainly — was it for a promotion? one Enclave agent did not answer on screen; the table established that advancement was riding on Lyra’s attempt. Vukradin offered to speak to Lyra’s superiors on her behalf, and one Enclave agent said that would be a bad idea, then explained why: the planar effects had been growing, Lyra thought this was their source and that she could stop them, and when she discovered the ritual she chose to implement it rather than take it to her bosses, who might have brought in people capable of performing it. Vukradin drew the conclusion — “She didn’t do what was best for the Emerald Enclave; she did what was best for her” — . another Enclave agent pushed back on behalf of their leader: she is a good boss and a good leader, she saw an opportunity, she saw everyone in the Enclave arguing over whether anything could or should be done, and she said she knew how to make it happen. “We trusted her. She’s been right in the past. She wasn’t this time.” Soma’s contribution — “You failed without our help” — drew looks from everyone at the table.

one Enclave agent then turned to Valphine and asked whether she had ever seen rituals as difficult as the one they attempted, which Soma notes is not a good thing to ask a drow. The answer: “Yes but they involve blood and death and torture.” Undeterred, he asked whether she was from the Underdark, and she said yes, from Menzoberranzan — and confirmed she is a cleric of Lathander. Her account of how that happened is that she came up to the Overbright to escape a little demon incursion in Menzoberranzan, thought it seemed like a good time to take some time off, and arrived to find it so bright that the sun burned her eyes and she said *wow, this is great*. She praised “The glorious painful brilliance of his light. It’s fantastic. I plan to stay in the Overbright for a while.” another Enclave agent, beside Soma, asked whether she harms herself and then uses healing spells as a kind of ritual as a speculative question about her beliefs. Valphine was confused by the premise: “It’s not a ritual?” she asked. “Is that not typical in the Overbright?” one Enclave agent, shocked, asked whether she does this to herself or to other people, and got a confused answer that did not resolve his concern in the slightest: “To whoever wants it.”

Soma steered back to the Enclave and asked why Lyra had chosen something so hard. one Enclave agent answered in pain: the Emerald Enclave had been arguing over whether to do anything at all — some saying they should intervene, some saying that intervening was itself the problem, others saying they should give it more time. Lyra had a particular affection for the Whispering Grove, was frustrated that nobody was doing anything, and so discovered the ritual and brought it to them, insisting that the forest and the gods would protect them and that they would succeed. They tried to explain that it was difficult. “But we didn’t and almost died.” With that, one Enclave agent and another Enclave agent rose to leave, and Vukradin asked whether they would like to join the party. They considered it for a moment, then declined: “We’ve made an oath to the Emerald Enclave.”

The evening closed with Ser Kaelen, who approached to ask about Vukradin’s work and plans and got a wary reception — Vukradin does not trust him. He asked the knight whether he had any information about the rightful owner of their treasures from the ship, and got a sigh and something muttered about how hard that is, which left him unconvinced. Ser Kaelen did praise his goal, which pleased him, since it is essential that everyone like him. Vukradin presented an updated ditty. Ser Kaelen called it an improvement but said it still did not capture the Order’s spirit and asked him to keep working.

The party chose Butterskull Ranch as its next destination and left quietly the next morning, unwilling to advertise another plan after their recent ambush. On the road they met a merchant returning from Conyberry. He had gone there expecting renewed trade after Lord Neverember’s promises, only to find an abandoned settlement: silent except for wind moving through burned and crumbling buildings. Vukradin immediately treated the old looting as a new restitution quest, insisting that the stolen property should be tracked down and returned to its owners’ next of kin.

At Conyberry the party found the ruins as described and searched for names, property, or anything else that might need returning. While they explored, they spotted a group of apparent bandits. Corbin recognized them as members of Carver’s gang. Vukradin urged him to approach; Corbin objected that people do not simply leave a gang, and the party began improvising a story that he had been recruiting them. The recording ends before the bandits respond.
